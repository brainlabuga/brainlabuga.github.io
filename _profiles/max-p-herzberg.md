Max P. Herzberg, Ph.D., is a developmental cognitive neuroscientist and Assistant Professor in the Department of Psychology at the University of Georgia. He also holds graduate faculty status in the Neuroscience program at UGA. Max directs the Brain Research in Adolescence and Infancy (BRAIN) Lab.

Dr. Herzberg’s research investigates the effects of stress on brain development and subsequent adaptive or maladaptive behavioral outcomes. In particular, he has long standing interest in the interactive effects of environments and brain structure and function in predicting risk for psychopathology and in exploring the effects of stress on neuroplasticity in human neuroimaging. The BRAIN lab leverages Dr. Herzberg’s expertise in structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to address these areas of interest and many more. Other methods used in the lab include electroencephalography (EEG), behavioral stress assessments, cognitive tasks, and self-reported behavioral data collection.

Max earned a bachelor’s degree in psychology with honors at Grinnell College where he was a Howard R. Bowen Scholar before attending the Institute of Child Development at the University of Minnesota where he earned a Ph.D. in Developmental Psychology. While at the University of Minnesota, Max was supported by a TL1 scholar training grant administered by the Clinical and Translational Science Institute and supported by the National Center for Advancing Translational Science (NCATS). Max completed postdoctoral training in the Department of Psychiatry at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, where he was a T32 scholar with support from NIMH and the Early Emotion Development Program. His research has been published in many high impact journals, including JAMA Pediatrics, Biological Psychiatry, PNAS, Neuropsychopharmacology, and Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience.

When Max isn’t in the lab, he enjoys running, golf, baking, and sitting on the back porch with his wife, Ellen. His toddler also provides many hours of fun—and is a source of endless half-baked research ideas.
